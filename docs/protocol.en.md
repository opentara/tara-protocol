# TARA Protocol

In development · Conceptual overview · 21 October 2026

Align the next step. Keep the differences visible.

We do not need to know everything or agree on everything to act together. We need to know which next step is acceptable, who may take it, within what limits, and when to reconsider.

TARA Protocol is a standard in development for coordinating action among people, AI systems and organizations under real-world conditions.

## Alignment without uniformity.

Interests cannot all be described in advance or aligned once and for all. A shared step can still be possible. Unknowns and disagreements remain explicit; consent, authority and commitments retain their force.

Sometimes the right next step is to clarify, negotiate, pause or decline. A promising outcome never substitutes for permission.

## Reality is not an edge case.

More capable systems still operate with partial knowledge, different perspectives and finite resources. We treat these as conditions of cooperation, rather than problems that can be solved away once and for all.

### 1. Knowledge is incomplete

An assumption can quietly become a fact.

**What can go wrong.** Neither a person nor an agent has a complete view of the situation. More information also costs time and attention.

**The proposed response.** Distinguish evidence, interpretation and unknowns. Seek information when it can change the next decision; state what remains uncertain.

### 2. People see different worlds

The same goal can mean different things.

**What can go wrong.** Agreement on a word such as “growth” can hide incompatible ideas of success, acceptable cost and a desirable future.

**The proposed response.** Make each relevant perspective and its criteria visible. Find a workable intersection without requiring a shared worldview.

### 3. Interests can genuinely conflict

One participant’s gain can be another’s cost.

**What can go wrong.** Better explanations do not always resolve a disagreement. Some benefits, burdens and opportunities cannot be shared equally.

**The proposed response.** Negotiate the conditions of a particular step. Record unresolved differences, who accepts which costs, and how participants can object or withdraw.

### 4. Understanding and execution can fail

A clear instruction can still produce the wrong action.

**What can go wrong.** A person can be misunderstood, an estimate can be wrong, or an implementation can behave differently from the plan.

**The proposed response.** Check what actually happened. Stop the affected action when needed, make the error visible and restore the ability to act responsibly.

### 5. The world and its participants change

Yesterday’s agreement may no longer fit.

**What can go wrong.** Intentions, circumstances and capabilities evolve. A new objective can also conflict with an existing commitment.

**The proposed response.** Set review triggers and revisit future action. Keep the history of decisions and commitments; do not silently rewrite it.

### 6. Resources are finite

Coordination itself has a cost.

**What can go wrong.** Time, money, attention, energy, expertise and computing capacity are limited. Repeated questions can consume the resource they were meant to protect.

**The proposed response.** Set a budget for the step and its coordination. Account for resources already committed, the cost of delay and a tolerable loss.

### 7. People are not internally consistent

A stated wish may be incomplete or provisional.

**What can go wrong.** Someone may want more opportunity and less workload at the same time, or discover what they want through experience.

**The proposed response.** Treat the initial formulation as a working understanding. Preserve tensions, offer a small informative trial and allow the person to revise their view.

### 8. Information and influence are unequal

Silence is not consent; access is not authority.

**What can go wrong.** Rank, fatigue, dependency and uneven access to information can distort apparent agreement. Some affected parties cannot speak for themselves.

**The proposed response.** Distinguish who knows, who decides and who bears the consequences. Keep refusal and correction available; make missing representation explicit.

### 9. Consequences extend beyond the target

A local success can create a wider harm.

**What can go wrong.** An improved metric can shift work, risk or cost to customers, other teams, communities or living systems.

**The proposed response.** Review benefits, burdens and unintended effects together. Identify who is affected but absent, and retain competing assessments of the result.

## One step. Explicit conditions. Room to revise.

The proposed unit of coordination is a feasible next step. Choose one leading aspect to work on now, while keeping the other constraints and commitments in view.

1. **See the situation** Who is involved or affected? What do they want to change or preserve? What is known, assumed, disputed or unavailable?

2. **Agree the conditions** What change is sufficient for this step? Who can authorize it? What are the limits on resources, risk and consequences?

3. **Take the permitted step** Clarify, negotiate, coordinate or execute. Use existing authority where it is sufficient; pause the part that exceeds it.

4. **Review and adapt** Compare the intended change with what happened, including costs and others’ assessments. Continue, revise, recover or stop.

A proposal is not a commitment. Consent is not inferred from silence. New information does not erase an earlier obligation.

## Faster actions. Faster consequences.

The stakes rise when agents act, coordinate and develop themselves. An unchecked assumption can pass from an answer into memory, a workflow and the decisions of other agents—before people have time to intervene.

- **An unknown becomes a “fact”.** A hallucination or unverified inference is reused as evidence. Repetition makes it look established.
- **One perspective becomes the goal.** An agent optimizes for one participant and silently drops objections, conflicting interests or costs borne by others.
- **A mistake becomes a working rule.** Changes to memory, instructions or workflows can preserve the mistake and multiply it across an organization.

Self-development needs a way to question what the system is learning—and authority over which changes it may adopt.

These are failure paths the proposed approach is designed to address. More capability or speed alone does not resolve them.

## Teach the agent. Govern the system.

Instructions are one layer. For an organization of agents, we propose explicit decision rights, enforceable limits and a process for accepting changes. A prompt cannot enforce these controls by itself.

### Who can decide?

Name the owner of a commitment, who may change it and whose objections must be considered. Existing authority supports autonomous work; a new goal does not silently replace an old obligation.

### What does the environment enforce?

Apply access controls, budgets and stop conditions in the execution environment. Keep a reachable escalation path and a way to suspend affected actions.

### What travels between agents?

Pass on the source, uncertainty, scope and unresolved objections alongside a claim or decision. A summary must not turn an assumption into evidence or silence into consent.

### How does a change become a rule?

Record the proposed change, evidence, affected commitments and authorized reviewer. Test it within a bounded scope, keep versions and a rollback path, and observe effects after adoption.

A current factual correction and a permanent change to behavior are different decisions. Self-development may change memory, instructions, tools or workflows; none of these changes proves improvement by itself.

## “Increase sales.” What should the agent do next?

Sales wants more orders. Finance protects margin. Support is at capacity. Customers need promises the organization can keep. Optimizing a sales metric alone does not settle what action is acceptable.

Illustrative scenario · The limits below are teaching examples, not results from a deployment.

### First, establish what the team knows.

- **Leading focus:** The CRM and payment records disagree. The team cannot yet tell which orders contribute to paid revenue.
- **Next step:** Compare an authorized sample of orders with payment records; document the gaps that remain.
- **Limits & authority:** Up to 30 minutes, read-only access. If the source is unavailable, mark it unknown rather than inventing an answer.
- **Review:** Is there now a usable basis for the next decision? Better data still does not authorize a discount.

### Then, negotiate a workable boundary.

- **Leading focus:** Sales proposes a discount. Finance rejects the margin loss. Support cannot absorb unrestricted growth.
- **Next step:** Present a limited offer and a no-discount alternative, with expected margin and support load, to the responsible decision-maker and affected teams.
- **Limits & authority:** Use the existing pricing rules until a change is authorized. A missing reply is not approval; unresolved objections stay in the record.
- **Review:** Has a specific test been authorized, with an owner, a capacity limit and stop conditions? If not, continue only already permitted work.

### Run the agreed test, then look beyond sales.

- **Leading focus:** An offer and its boundaries have been explicitly approved. The immediate task is now execution.
- **Next step:** Run only the agreed test, record what happened and compare paid orders, margin, support load and customer feedback.
- **Limits & authority:** Stay within the authorized audience, duration and capacity. Pause affected work if a limit is exceeded or authority is withdrawn.
- **Review:** Decide whether to continue, change or stop. A rise in orders alone does not establish a good outcome for the whole system.

The parties have not acquired identical interests. They have made the conditions of one shared step explicit.

### The same question, without AI.

A community shares a room. Some members want evening events; neighbors need quiet; volunteers have limited time. A one-month schedule, explicit quiet hours and a joint review can make a trial possible. Disagreement over the long-term use of the space may remain.

## A common language for cooperation.

TARA connects concepts, coordination and experience. Domain standards and methods give them a concrete setting; software implementations must make the limits enforceable.

- **TARA Protocol & language.** The language describes participants, interests, uncertainty, resources, constraints, commitments, steps and experience. The protocol develops how participants coordinate and revise action using these concepts.
- **Domain standards.** Open Digital Employee describes digital roles, context, authority and outcomes. Open Human Context concerns the partial, revisable representation of a person available to an agent. Each keeps its own specification and release process.
- **Methods & frameworks.** Choose a method for the actual difficulty: systems inquiry for different perspectives, facilitation for disagreement, a small experiment for uncertainty. Adaptations preserve their sources and require testing in their new setting.
- **Implementation & evaluation.** Tools execute actions, apply access controls and budgets, and collect evidence. A valid record is only one check; whether action was useful, harmful or appropriately authorized must be evaluated separately.

This page explains the current proposal. It is not a released normative specification or evidence that a universal TARA runtime already works.
